\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning has been increasingly applied to competitive environments in which
an opponent's behaviour is not fixed. When an opponent's behaviour changes abruptly, the
resulting non-stationarity can weaken a previously effective policy. Hierarchical architectures are broadly expected to re-adapt faster
than flat architectures under these circumstances, yet this expectation has barely been tested
specifically against a shifting opponent. This work investigates whether a
hierarchical Feudal Network (FuN) recovers more quickly than a flat Proximal
Policy Optimization (PPO) agent following abrupt opponent strategy shifts in the
game of Go. Both
agents are trained against fixed heuristic opponents in PettingZoo's
\texttt{go\_v5} on $9\times9$ and $13\times13$ boards, after which mid-training
opponent shifts of varying magnitude and frequency are introduced. The effect of these shifts is measured by recovery time, along with complementary measures of depth and overall cost of the resulting performance drop. Across the conditions examined, the hierarchical agent does not recover faster than the flat agent.
These findings suggest that the FuN hierarchy does not provide the expected advantage under opponent-induced non-stationarity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

%% Motivate: real opponents change strategy mid-interaction - the agent's
%% policy suddenly underperforms and must re-adapt (non-stationarity).
Reinforcement learning (RL) is a framework in which an agent learns how to
behave by interacting with an environment. The agent observes different states, takes actions on those states and receives a reward. From this the agent gradually adjusts its behaviour to maximise these rewards it collects over time~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. Through repeated trial and
error the agent learns a policy, a function that maps each state to an action. A policy works well as long as the environment stays the same but many real settings are not static. In
competitive games an opponent does not play the same way forever and may change its strategy as the game unfolds. When the opponent changes, the
conditions the agent originally learned against no longer hold, so behaviour
that was once effective can suddenly perform poorly. The effect of a shifting environment caused by an opponent's behaviour changing, is
known as non-stationarity~\cite{padakandla2022survey}. This is the central difficulty the study addresses. An agent that cannot adjust quickly to a variable opponent suffers a lasting drop in performance. This motivates investigating how the
architecture of a learning agent affects how quickly it recovers once an
opponent shifts strategy.

%% The idea under test: a manager/worker hierarchy should recover faster.
A learning agent is commonly implemented as a flat policy, which is a single network that maps observations directly to actions. A widely used example of this design is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)~\cite{schulman2015trust}. When the opponent shifts, a flat agent has to
revise the single policy it learned as a whole. This is slow and risks overwriting
behaviour that is still useful. An alternative design is a hierarchical agent, which
splits decision-making across levels. The Feudal Network (FuN)~\cite{dayan1993feudal, vezhnevets2017feudal} is one instance of this structure. The architecture has a high-level manager setting goals over a longer timescale while a low-level worker chooses the primitive actions that pursue them. The idea this research tests is that the hierarchy should recover faster after an opponent changes strategy. The manager can switch to a new high-level plan while the worker keeps the low-level skills it has already acquired. This minimizes relearning compared to a flat agent. However, this advantage has not been demonstrated with an opponent that changes strategy during game-play.

The gap of whether the hierarchical structure of learning agents adapts to changing strategic opponents motivates the focused research question of this work: \emph{Does hierarchical feudal reinforcement learning recover faster than flat PPO after abrupt opponent strategy shifts?} The question is studied in
the board game Go, where a hierarchical Feudal Network and a flat PPO agent are
each trained against fixed heuristic opponents and then subjected to abrupt
strategy shifts, on two board sizes, $9\times9$ and $13\times13$.

The study is built around three main elements. First, a controlled benchmark is
constructed for measuring recovery from opponent strategy shifts in Go. The timing, magnitude and frequency of each shift are fixed in advance so that recovery can be measured cleanly. Second, a Feudal Network and a flat PPO agent
are compared under identical conditions, matched across shift magnitude,
frequency and board size. Any difference in recovery therefore reflects the architecture rather than the experimental setup. Third, recovery is assessed
with three supporting metrics, each capturing a different aspect of the
process.

%% Main finding: the hierarchy did not improve recovery.
The focused elements the study is built around yield a direct test of how quickly a learning agent recovers a policy once a shift in an opponent's strategy has made it ineffective. It establishes whether the recovery advantage expected of the hierarchy holds when a disruption is introduced into the learning environment. The findings of the research showed that this advantage did not materialise, instead favouring the flat baseline in adaptation capability. The sections that follow develop this result and examine why it arises.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning in Go}
Go is an ancient two-player board game that originated in China more than two
thousand years ago and is still globally played. The players take turns placing
black and white stones on the intersections of a square grid. Both players can see the whole board at all times, so no
information is hidden from either side. A stone is captured and removed once opposing stones occupy every point beside it. The winner is the
player who controls the larger share of the board at the end. This is decided by
area scoring, which counts both the territory a player surrounds and the stones
it has in play~\cite{silver2016mastering}. Moving first is an advantage, so a
fixed number of points called komi is added to the second player's score to keep
the game balanced. A game continues until both players pass in succession, by which point a typical game has run to a few hundred moves.

Although its rules are simple, Go is extremely hard to play well. For many years
this difficulty made it a landmark challenge for artificial intelligence. At each
turn a player has a very large number of legal moves to choose from. A full game
lasts hundreds of turns, giving Go a space of possible games far larger than in
chess~\cite{silver2016mastering}.
Go demands long-term planning since a stone placed early can prove decisive
many moves later. The search-based programs that had already mastered chess could
not cope with this scale, as there are far too many moves to examine~\cite{campbell2002deepblue}. Progress
instead came from reinforcement learning.

The breakthrough came with AlphaGo, which combined neural networks with look-ahead search and millions of games of self-play to beat a professional human player~\cite{silver2016mastering}, a milestone that drew broad attention to reinforcement learning. Systems like AlphaGo however, were trained under stable conditions improving against themselves or against fixed opponents rather
than against an opponent that changes strategy midway through a game. This study
uses Go differently. Go is a game of perfect information, with no hidden state or
element of chance. Any difference in how two agents handle a mid-game change of
opponent can be traced to their architecture and not to information they could not see.

%% non-stationary and why shifts effect policies
\subsection{Opponent-Induced Non-stationarity}
\subsubsection{Non-stationarity from a Changing Opponent}
In standard reinforcement learning the environment is assumed to be stationary.
Its transition and reward dynamics stay fixed throughout
learning~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. When
an agent trains against an opponent, it learns to exploit the opponent's behaviour. If the opponent later plays differently, those dynamics change. It means the experience the agent has gathered no longer describes the game in front of it~\cite{littman1994markov}. This shifting of the environment is what is meant by non-stationarity and learning to cope with it has been a recurring focus of reinforcement learning research~\cite{busoniu2010overview, padakandla2022survey}.

\subsubsection{Why a Strategy Shift Breaks a Learned Policy}
A policy is only as good as the conditions it was trained under. Through repeated
game-play the agent settles on actions that worked well against the previous opponent. It does this through value estimates that reflect how that opponent responded. When the
opponent shifts, the same positions begin to arise from different play. Actions
that were once rewarded may no longer be. The agent's value estimates also stop
matching what it now observes. Performance therefore drops at the moment of the
shift. The metric only recovers once the agent has gathered enough
experience of the new opponent to correct its policy~\cite{he2016opponent}. The speed of that recovery is the focused concern of this work. It is shaped in large part by how the agent
is built.

\subsection{Flat and Hierarchical RL}
%%Flat on-policy RL: PPO - the baseline under test.
\subsubsection{Flat Policies and PPO}
The simplest way to build a learning agent is through a flat policy. A single network
maps each observation directly to a choice of action. This has no internal division
of the decision into levels. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a recognised used
method of this kind~\cite{schulman2015trust}. PPO is an on-policy algorithm that
improves the framework in small steps to keep learning stable. This design is simple
and effective but it holds all of the agent's behaviour in one set of
parameters. When the opponent shifts, the agent cannot change part of its
behaviour while keeping the rest fixed. It has to revise the entire policy at once.
Useful skills can be overwritten in the process. This is the weakness that a
hierarchical design tries to avoid.

%% Hierarchical RL and Feudal Networks: Manager--Worker, temporal abstraction, intrinsic sub-goal reward; note the claims that it adapts to a different opponent.
\subsubsection{Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning and Feudal Networks}
Hierarchical reinforcement learning takes a different approach. It divides control
across two levels of decision-making~\cite{pateria2021survey}. A
high-level policy plans over the long term by setting sub-goals, while a
low-level policy chooses the individual moves that work towards them. The low-level policy is
guided by an intrinsic reward for reaching each sub-goal~\cite{kulkarni2016hierarchical}.
A sub-goal covers many moves, so the high level policy makes a lot less decisions
than the low level policy makes moves. This compression of the problem is known as temporal
abstraction~\cite{sutton1999between}. The Feudal Network (FuN) is a neural
instance of this idea, built on early feudal reinforcement
learning~\cite{dayan1993feudal}. A Manager sets a high-level goal at intervals and a 
worker acts at every step to follow it~\cite{vezhnevets2017feudal}. This
separation is what makes the structure appealing for a variable opponent. Due to
the Manager being able to adopt a new plan while the Worker keeps its low-level skills.

\subsection{Measuring Adaptation and Recovery}
%% Recovery-time / adaptation-speed metrics; how the recovery measure relates and contrasts with catastrophic-forgetting work.
The effect of a change in the environment is measured by how quickly an agent returns to its former
level of performance. This is usually called recovery time or adaptation
speed~\cite{alshedivat2018continuous}. It is related to work on catastrophic
forgetting but the two differ. Forgetting deals with how much of a previously
learned skill is lost when a network is trained on a new task~\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}. Recovery on the other hand concerns how quickly
current performance returns once an opponent has changed. This study tracks
performance as a rolling win rate over a fixed window of games. Recovery is
measured as the number of games the win rate stays below a set fraction of its
pre-shift value. This is supported by other measures of how far the metric falls and how much is lost while it stays there. These measures are defined in the experimental design to follow.

It has contrasted flat and hierarchical agents and shown how recovery is measured. These lines of work have not yet been brought together. How a hierarchical agent recovers when an opponent changes strategy has rarely been examined directly. Plus it has seldom been set against a flat agent under matched conditions. This study provides that comparison, using recovery from a controlled opponent shift in Go as its test. The next section sets how this comparison is designed and carried out.

\subsection{Environment and Opponents}
%% \textbf{Environment:} PettingZoo \texttt{go\_v5}; $9\times9$ (komi 5.5) and $13\times13$ (komi 7.5); area scoring. Note the move-cap / forced-termination handling.

\subsubsection{Environment}
All experiments are run in \texttt{go\_v5}, the Go environment from the PettingZoo
library~\cite{terry2021pettingzoo}. This provides a standard and reliable
implementation of the complete rules of play. In each game the trained agent controls
one colour while a fixed opponent controls the other. Two board sizes are studied as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:boards}. The first is a $9\times9$ board
with a komi of 5.5 and the second is a $13\times13$ board with a komi of 7.5. Komi
adds a fixed number of points to the second player's score to offset the
advantage of moving first. These
sizes balance strategic depth against the cost of training. This is because a smaller board
shortens each game and allows many more to be played within a fixed budget of training steps. 

At each turn the agent receives the board as a stack of seventeen binary planes of
size $N\times N$, where $N$ is the board dimension (9 or 13). These planes record the stones of both players over recent moves along
with the colour to play~\cite{silver2017mastering}. This is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:obs-encoding}. For the agents own move it chooses among the $N\times N$ intersections or a single pass.

Two design choices ensure that every game ends in a clean and decisive result.
First, the opponents are built to keep playing while valuable moves remain rather than passing early. In Go two passes end the game and the board is then scored exactly as it stands. If an opponent passed while territory was still contested it would end the game in an unfinished position. This would make the result depend on when it stopped rather than on the quality of play. By playing the position out, each
opponent forces a full contest for territory, so the agent must genuinely control
more of the board to win. Second, we impose a fixed cap on the number of moves
per game. This prevents the rare case where two deterministic players could
continue indefinitely under area scoring. When the cap is reached, both players
are forced to pass. This makes the environment always return a final score. Together
these choices give every game a clear area result, which provides the consistent
performance signal the recovery analysis depends on.

\subsubsection{Fixed Opponents}

The agents are trained against a set of five fixed opponents, each following a
simple hand-coded strategy. Every opponent plays deterministically and reacts
only to the current board. This makes the behaviour stable and consistent. Due to this
it possible to change opponents at certain points during training and to be confident
any change in performance is from that switch.

The five styles differ in what they value, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:opponents}. The greedy opponent takes the largest
available area on each move and attacks only when a capture is offered. It builds thin, loosely held positions and does not hunt agent's stones.
The defensive opponent focuses on its own groups, connecting stones and
protecting liberties while avoiding needless contact. The corner opponent
plays logical contact moves with a lean towards one corner of the board. The
edge opponent behaves similarly but leans towards the sides. The random opponent
selects a legal move uniformly at random on every turn. It serves as a baseline
with no strategy at all. The four strategic opponents are deliberately imperfect.
They follow their chosen style but do not avoid basic tactical mistakes, which
keeps each one beatable by a trained agent. Figure~\ref{fig:opponents} shows a
representative position for each style. Their difference is visible by where each opponent chooses to play. This is shown by the move-density
heatmaps in Figure~\ref{fig:opponent-heatmaps}.

\subsection{Agents}
%% \textbf{Agents:} PPO (action-masked categorical policy, shared CNN backbone); FuN (matched CNN perception, Manager dilated-LSTM trained on extrinsic reward, Worker trained on intrinsic cosine reward). Two agents are compared, a flat agent and a hierarchical one. Both take the same
board observation and pass it through the same lower network. This shared network,
called the backbone is a convolutional neural network (CNN) that converts the raw board into a compact internal representation. The agents also share the same observation preprocessing. The only
difference between them is the structure placed on top of this backbone. Any
difference in behaviour can be traced to the architecture rather
than to the input or the backbone itself. Figure~\ref{fig:agents} below shows an example of a high level view the two different agents architecture.

\subsubsection{Flat Agent (PPO)}
The flat agent is a single network trained with Proximal Policy
Optimization~\cite{schulman2015trust}. \footnote{The PPO
implementation is adapted from
\url{https://github.com/adi3e08/PPO}} From the internal representation of the CNN backbone, the network produces two
outputs. The first is a probability distribution over the possible moves, which
the agent looks at to decide where to play. The second is an estimate of how
favourable the current position is, which guides learning. Before a move is
sampled, illegal actions are removed so that only legal placements or a pass can be
selected~\cite{huang2022masking}. Due to everything sitting in one network, a change in strategy has to be
learned by updating the whole policy at once.

\subsubsection{Hierarchical Agent (Feudal Network)}
The hierarchical agent is a Feudal Network~\cite{vezhnevets2017feudal,
dayan1993feudal}. \footnote{The Feudal Network implementation is adapted from
\url{https://github.com/lweitkamp/feudalnets-pytorch}.} It shares the same backbone as the flat agent then splits into
a Manager and a Worker. The Manager acts less often and sets a goal as a direction
in the network's latent space. It uses a dilated long short-term memory network, known as a dilated LSTM. This retains information over many steps~\cite{hochreiter1997lstm}. From this alone it learns the game outcome. The Worker acts at every step by selecting moves
that best follow the Manager's current goal. It is trained by an intrinsic reward
that measures how closely the change in the agent's state matches the direction
the Manager sets. Due to this signal arriving at every step, the Worker keeps
learning even though the game's own reward comes only at the end.

Several corrections were needed before the hierarchy trained correctly.  In the
implementation inherited at the start of the study, the Manager's goal was
detached from the computation graph on every step. This removed the gradient the
Manager needed to learn useful goals, so the hierarchy stayed inactive. The same
handling also limited the dilated memory to a single step. Once the goal gradient
and the recurrent memory were restored, the Manager began to set meaningful goals. The key hyperparameters are listed in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}. PPO uses widely adopted default settings drawn from standard algorithm implementations~\cite{andrychowicz2021what}. The FuN values follow the Feudal Network~\cite{vezhnevets2017feudal} and the code reference implementation. A small number of FuN values were adjusted for stable training in the single-worker Go setting. The learning rate was lowered to $2\times10^{-4}$, the manager discount to $0.995$ and the intrinsic weight to $0.5$. Shared values are identical across both agents. The two agents also differed in capacity. FuN had far more parameters
than PPO, roughly 26$\times$ on $9\times9$ and 47$\times$ on $13\times13$(Table~\ref{tab:paramcount}).

\subsubsection{External Reference (GNU Go)}
The five heuristic opponents are tailor made so they give no absolute sense of how strong the trained agents are. As a reference point, GNU
Go is played against each opponent. GNU Go is an established rule-based engine that
plays Go without learning~\cite{gnugo}. On both board sizes it beats every
opponent by a wide margin (Table~\ref{tab:gnugo}), which fixes a strong upper reference for play against
them. The trained agents are then read against this reference. It shows whether
each agent reaches the level of a competent engine on the same opponents or falls
short.

%% \textbf{Phase 1:} train each agent against each opponent to a stable win-rate baseline; this baseline defines the recovery target.
\subsubsection{Phase 1: Baseline Training}
Phase 1 measures how well each agent can learn to play a single fixed opponent.
Each agent is trained from scratch against each of the five opponents, one
opponent per run. Both agents use identical training settings and the same step budget, so the only difference between them remains the
architecture.

Performance during training is tracked as a rolling win rate over recent games.
This win rate climbs as the agent learns the opponent then settles once further
gains are small. The settled value is taken as the agent's baseline against that
opponent. This baseline sets the target for Phase 2, where the same agent must
recover to it after the opponent changes.

\subsubsection{Phase 2: Opponent Shifts}
Phase 2 introduces the abrupt opponent shifts the study is built around, illustrated as part of the pipeline in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}. Each run resumes a stable
Phase-1 checkpoint, so the agent begins from a known baseline against a known
opponent. Midway through that opponent is replaced by a different one. Every
shift is between two opponents that both agents can already beat, so a drop in
performance reflects the disruption of the change itself rather than a jump to an
unbeatable opponent. Training continues after the shift and the agent is left to
recover on its own.

\paragraph{Shift magnitude.} Three magnitudes are used, which are set by how much disruption
each one causes. The size of a shift follows from how
far apart the two opponents' styles are. A LOW shift moves from the corner opponent
to the edge opponent. Both actively contest position and differ only in the region
of the board they favour. A MED shift moves from the corner
opponent to the defensive opponent. This replaces an aggressive, position-fighting
style with a cautious one that consolidates its own groups and avoids
confrontation. A HIGH shift moves from the greedy opponent to
the defensive opponent. The greedy opponent seizes the largest open areas of the
board, while the defensive opponent holds firm and refuses exchanges. The two demand very different responses, which makes this the most disruptive shift and the hardest to recover from. Table~\ref{tab:magnitudes} summarises these magnitudes.

\paragraph{Shift frequency.} Frequency controls how often the opponent changes
during a run. A single change tests plain recovery. Repeated changes test whether
the agent can keep adapting when the environment will not settle. Three frequency levels are studied, labelled f1, f2 and f3. At f1 the
opponent shifts once, which isolates recovery from a single disruption. At f2 it
shifts a few times at regular intervals, so the agent must recover more than once.
At f3 it shifts many times in quick succession, leaving little time to settle
between changes. Crossed with magnitude, this gives nine shift conditions. Figure~\ref{fig:shift-schedule} shows the three schedules on the $9\times9$
board.


\paragraph{Design.} Each condition is run for both agents on both board sizes and
repeated over three random seeds. Crossing all of these gives 108 Phase-2 runs.
The seeds share the same Phase-1 starting point, so they capture the variability
of Phase 2 rather than the initial training. Each run records the outcome of
every game. Recovery is then measured from these records using the metrics
defined in the next section to follow.

%% \textbf{Parameters:} exact shift schedules and step budgets.
\subsubsection{Parameters}
Every run is given a fixed budget of environment steps, where one step is a single
move by the agent. The budgets differ by board size because the larger board is
slower to learn. On the $9\times9$ board, Phase 1 trains for three million steps
and Phase 2 for 2.3 million. On the $13\times13$ board these rise to five
million and four million, about $1.7$ times larger in each phase. Scaling both
phases together means an agent has a comparable amount of time to reach its
baseline and to recover, whichever board it plays. Without this the recovery on the
slower board could be cut off before it finished.

A shift schedule lists the opponent the agent faces and the exact step at which
each change happens. Each Phase-2 run loads a Phase-1 checkpoint then follows its
schedule while training continues. The first change is placed early, once the
resumed agent's rolling win rate has settled back to its Phase-1 level against the starting
opponent. This is at
300\,000 steps on the $9\times9$ board and 500\,000 on the $13\times13$ board. The
three frequencies then differ in how many changes follow. At f1 the opponent
changes once and later changes back. At f2 it changes a few times at even spacing.
At f3 it changes ten times in steady succession. Due to every schedule being fixed
beforehand, both agents and all seeds face exactly the same sequence of changes at
exactly the same steps.



\subsection{Metrics}
%% \textbf{Metrics used:} Rolling win rate (window $100$ games); recovery time; dip depth; adaptation cost. Description and purpose of each metric.
Performance is measured throughout by a rolling win rate. This is the fraction of
the last one hundred games that the agent won. A single game is too noisy to read
on its own, so the rolling average gives a stable signal. The value of this signal
on the game just before a shift is taken as the pre-shift baseline. Recovery is
judged against a band set at $0.8$ times that baseline. Three further measures then
describe how often a shift disrupts the agent and what happens once it does. Figure~\ref{fig:metrics} shows all four measures marked on one real recovery segment.

\paragraph{Recovery time.} Recovery time is the primary measure. It applies the
idea of adaptation speed from earlier work to a shifting
opponent~\cite{alshedivat2018continuous}. It is the number of games the rolling
win rate stays below the $0.8$ band, counted from the game it first drops below to
the game it first returns. A shorter recovery time means the agent re-adapts
faster. If a shift never pushes the win rate below the band, its recovery time is
zero. If the win rate never climbs back above the band, the agent has not
recovered. Recovery time is then counted to the end of that shift's segment.

\paragraph{Dip depth.} Dip depth measures how hard performance falls. It is the
gap between the pre-shift baseline and the lowest rolling win rate reached after
the shift. Large drops of this kind are a central concern when an agent must cope
with a changing environment~\cite{padakandla2022survey}. Dip depth captures
severity rather than speed, so a shift can score a deep dip even when the agent
recovers quickly.

\paragraph{Adaptation cost.} Adaptation cost combines depth and duration into a
single number. It is the total performance lost while the win rate sits below the
baseline. It is measured as the area between the baseline and the rolling win rate over
the dip. This is a form of the cumulative loss an agent incurs while it
re-adapts~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. A deep, short dip and a shallow, long
dip can carry the same cost. It therefore gives an overall measure of the burden a
shift imposes. 

\paragraph{Disruption rate.}
After each shift, we check whether the rolling win rate drops below the $0.8$ band at any point afterward. If it does the shift counts as a disruption. It is taken as the number of shifts that count as a disruption, divided by the total number of shifts.

\end{figure}



\subsection{Tools}
%% \textbf{Setup:} GPU and CPU training on the UCT cluster and CPU training on the CHPC cluster; metrics logged to Weights \& Biases and to per-game CSV files; per-seed runs kept separate then merged; state the number of seeds.
Both agents were implemented in PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} and shared a
single training pipeline. Training was carried out on two high-performance
computing clusters. The University of Cape Town cluster provided GPU and CPU nodes, as well as the CHPC cluster, which provided additional CPU capacity. The full compute and software stack is summarised in Table~\ref{tab:tools}.

Each run produced two records. The first was a live stream of metrics to Weights
and Biases. The second was a per-game log recording the outcome of every game.
These per-game logs served as the source for all recovery measures, which were
computed offline after training. Every seed was run and stored separately then
the seeds were merged at analysis time to produce a mean and a standard error.
Three seeds were used for each Phase-2 condition. The full implementation is
available online.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/bradrscott/honours_rl_project}}

\section{Discussion}

%%  Open with: recap what was tested, then interpret the Results.
This study compared a Feudal Network (FuN) agent against a flat PPO agent under controlled opponent strategy shifts in Go. Both agents were first trained against five fixed opponents on two board sizes, $9\times9$ and $13\times13$, to a stable win-rate baseline. Each agent then faced an abrupt mid-training change in opponent strategy. This change was varied in magnitude and in frequency. Recovery time was the primary measure of each agent's response to a shift. Three further measures of disruption rate, dip depth, and adaptation cost complemented it.

The results reject the expectation that a hierarchical architecture improves recovery time. The flat agent recovered faster than the hierarchical agent in every board-magnitude combination. The hierarchical agent was also disrupted more often. Its win rate dropped further during a disruption and its adaptation cost was higher. This pattern held on both boards, across every condition tested. The hierarchy did not protect against the opponent shift, it increased exposure to it.

\medskip

%% Does the result answer RQ3 / reject the hypothesis that hierarchy speeds recovery?
The research question asks whether the feudal architecture recovers faster than the flat architecture after an opponent shift. The evidence gives a clear negative result. The introduction proposed that a manager-worker split spares an agent from a full policy rewrite. That information finds no support here as the the flat agent recovers faster than the hierarchy in every condition tested.

\medskip

%% Analyse \emph{why} the result is negative - candidate mechanisms.
Two features of the results explain why the hierarchy failed to help. The first is present before any shift occurs. Phase 1 shows that the hierarchical agent reached a weaker and less decisive baseline than the flat agent. Its final win rates were lower against nearly every opponent, dropping as low as 54\% where the flat agent approached 100\% (Table~\ref{tab:phase1}). Its games were also longer in all ten conditions and close to three times longer against the corner opponent (Figure~\ref{fig:phase1-eplen}). A longer game is less decisive and reflects a policy that wins by a narrow margin rather than a wide one. This is because it gives less room to absorb disruption. A strategy change that a dominant policy would withstand can push a marginal policy below its own baseline. This is visible in the disruption rate. The hierarchical agent was knocked below its recovery band in 52\% to 75\% percent of shifts, versus 0\% to 27\% percent for the flat agent (Figure~\ref{fig:phase2-disruption}). The recovery band is set relative to each agent's own baseline, so this gap reflects genuine fragility rather than a lower starting point.

The second feature concerns the adaptation itself. The expected benefit assumes that a shift can be absorbed by the manager alone, leaving the worker's skills intact~\cite{vezhnevets2017feudal}. This assumption does not hold under an opponent shift. A change in opponent strategy alters the moves the worker faces at every step and not only the high-level plan the manager selects. Both levels are forced to re-adapt at once. The manager must find a new goal and the worker must relearn how to pursue the goal against a different opponent. The two levels are coupled through the manager's goal signal, so neither can settle until the other does~\cite{dayan1993feudal}. The manager also operates on a slower time scale, which gives it fewer reformative updates per game and slows the search for a new goal. The structure intended to localise the update instead distributes it across two learners that must realign together. This is consistent with the recovery times in Table~\ref{tab:phase2}, where the hierarchical agent takes hundreds of games to recover on the $9\times9$ board while the flat agent recovers within tens.

\medskip

%% How recovery depends on magnitude, frequency and board size, and what the two boards reveal about the metrics themselves (recovery time is informative on $9\times9$; dip depth / adaptation cost on $13\times13$).
Recovery decreased for the hierarchical agent as the magnitude of the shift increased. Every measure moved in the same direction. On the $9\times9$ board its mean recovery time rose from 92 games at low magnitude to 693 at high magnitude. Its adaptation cost rose from 18.9 to 100.5 thousand steps over the same range. The $13\times13$ board showed the same unvarying increase in dip depth and adaptation cost (Table~\ref{tab:phase2}). The flat agent showed no comparable trend. Its adaptation cost on $13\times13$ stayed between 17 and 19 thousand steps regardless of magnitude. A larger strategy change imposed a larger burden on the hierarchy alone.

Shift frequency acted through repetition rather than depth. The aggregated measures in Table~\ref{tab:phase2} pool the three frequencies, so the effect is seen in the rolling curves (Figures~\ref{fig:phase2-curves-9x9} and~\ref{fig:phase2-curves-13x13}). A single shift produced one dip. Frequent shifts produced a dip after each change. This is clearest in the high-magnitude row under the f3 schedule where the hierarchical agent fell after each successive shift without fully settling between them. The flat agent produced only shallow dips at the same points.

The two boards did not agree on which measure of recovery was most informative. On the $9\times9$ board recovery time separated the agents sharply by up to an order of magnitude. On the $13\times13$ board this separation narrowed. The flat agent's recovery reached 48 games at high magnitude, which overlaps the hierarchical agent's range of 21 to 104 (Table~\ref{tab:phase2}). Recovery time counted in games compresses on the larger board because its games are longer and its win-rate signal moves more slowly across them. Dip depth and adaptation cost do not suffer this compression. They separated the agents clearly on $13\times13$ where the hierarchical agent reached a dip of 0.27 and a cost of 120 thousand steps against the flat agent's 0.17 and 19. No single measure was sufficient on both boards. This is the reason the study reports three complementary measures rather than relying on recovery time alone.

\medskip

%% How the finding aligns with or contrasts the related literature (HRL's claimed recovery advantage vs an opponent-shift setting).
The results run against the advantage that hierarchical reinforcement learning is usually credited with but the contrast is narrower than it first appears. That advantage was established in a particular regime. Feudal Networks were shown to transfer quickly when moved to a new single-agent game~\cite{vezhnevets2017feudal}. Hierarchical methods more broadly were established to help with sparse rewards and long horizons~\cite{kulkarni2016hierarchical, sutton1999between}. In each case the benefit came from temporal abstraction over a fixed environment. The present result does not contradict those advantageous findings. It shows that the hierarchy does not extend to an opponent that changes strategy. Where a transfer to a new game is reported to be fast, a transfer to a new opponent here was slow. Even at low magnitude on the $9\times9$ board the hierarchical agent needed 92 games to recover, against 6 for the flat agent (Table~\ref{tab:phase2}).

The reason the extension fails lies in what each kind of change disturbs. When a hierarchical agent transfers to a new task, the agents goals change while the environment dynamics stay intact. The workers low level skills remain valid and the manager redirects them toward the new goal. An opponent shift however, changes the dynamics themselves. The moves the worker faces are no longer the moves it was trained against, so its low-level skill is no longer reliable and cannot simply be redirected. Two results support this reading. First, the hierarchical agent entered each shift from a less decisive baseline with games up to three times longer than the flat agent's (Figure~\ref{fig:phase1-eplen}).Longer games against opponents both agents can beat point to less commanding play. FuN won by narrow margins rather than from dominant positions, leaving it with a smaller advantage to fall back on when the opponent changed. Second, when the opponent changed the hierarchical agents policy gave way rather than holding. It was disrupted on 52\% to 75\% of shifts and fell to dips of up to 0.30 in win rate against 0 to 27 percent and 0.17 for the flat agent (Figure~\ref{fig:phase2-disruption}, Table~\ref{tab:phase2}). The worker did not retain reliable skills across the shift, which is precisely what the hierarchical advantage assumes. The way prior work splits along these lines reflects this difficulty. Adaptation to shifting opponents has been pursued mainly through flat policies and opponent modelling~\cite{alshedivat2018continuous, he2016opponent}, while the hierarchical case had seen little direct test~\cite{vezhnevets2017feudal, pateria2021survey}. The gap that this study set out to close was real and the result within it is that the hierarchy offered no recovery advantage under a direct opponent shift.

\medskip

%% \textbf{Implications:} what the negative result means for using HRL under opponent-induced non-stationarity, and when a hierarchy might still help.
The practical implication is direct. A manager-worker hierarchy should not be adopted for its supposed robustness to a changing opponent. In this study it carried the added complexity of a manager and a worker in place of a single network as well as recovered more slowly on every measure. The disadvantage was not fixed in size, it widened as the shift grew more severe. On the $13\times13$ board the flat agent's adaptation cost stayed near 17 to 19 thousand steps across all three magnitudes, while the hierarchical agent's rose from 37 to 76 to 120 thousand (Figure~\ref{fig:phase2-dipadapt}). The hierarchy was weakest in precisely the conditions where robustness matters most. The flat agent by contrast, absorbed mild shifts almost completely. It was never once disrupted at low magnitude on the $13\times13$ board (Figure~\ref{fig:phase2-disruption}). For opponent-induced non-stationarity of the kind tested here, a flat policy is the safer default.

This does not rule out a hierarchy in every case, though the results offer only limited positive evidence. One genuine exception stands out. The single Phase-1 condition in which the hierarchical agent surpassed the flat agent was the edge opponent on the $13\times13$ board, where it reached 87\% against the flat agent's 68.5\% (Table~\ref{tab:phase1}). This is also the opponent introduced by the low-magnitude shift, the setting the hierarchy came closest to matching the flat agent. Its dip depth on $13\times13$ was 0.16 at low magnitude against 0.27 at high (Table~\ref{tab:phase2}). This was the shallowest it recorded. Where the change was mild and the low-level policy was already competent against the incoming opponent, the hierarchy was least outmatched. This aligns with the condition under which its advantage is expected to hold. A hierarchy may help when a shift leaves the worker's skills largely valid like in a transfer to a new task~\cite{vezhnevets2017feudal}. It may also help in settings with long horizons or sparse rewards where temporal abstraction is decisive~\cite{kulkarni2016hierarchical, sutton1999between}. An opponent shift of any real magnitude meets none of these. Establishing where the boundary lies is a question for further work.

\medskip

%% \textbf{Limitations:} all seeds start from the same Phase-1 model, so they capture only Phase-2 randomness; win rate used rather than a territory/score margin; boards limited to $9\times9$ and $13\times13$, not full-size $19\times19$ Go.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. The first concerns the seed design. All three Phase-2 seeds resume from the same Phase-1 model. This means the standard errors reported in Table~\ref{tab:phase2} capture only the randomness of the shift process not the variance of Phase-1 training. A different Phase-1 run could reach a different baseline and respond differently to a shift. The Phase-1 results themselves derive from a single run per condition (Table~\ref{tab:phase1}) and carry no variance estimate at all.

The second is the size of that uncertainty. The standard errors on recovery time are large relative to the means, reaching 214 games on a mean of 693 and 146 on a mean of 316 (Table~\ref{tab:phase2}). The flat agent was rarely disrupted (Figure~\ref{fig:phase2-disruption}) so its recovery-time means rest on few events and are correspondingly noisy. Its recovery on the $9\times9$ board does not increase steadily with magnitude, it measures 6, 22, and 18 games across the three levels. Despite this noise in the the numbers, the direction of the findings is consistent across all four measures and both boards. The precise magnitudes should be read with this uncertainty in mind.

The third is the performance measure. Recovery is judged by win rate, which is a binary record of the game outcome, rather than by a territory or score margin. Win rate captures whether a game was won, not by how much. The Phase-1 game lengths already indicate that margin varies where win rate does not (Figure~\ref{fig:phase1-eplen}). A score-based measure might expose disruption that the win-rate signal absorbs.

The fourth focuses on scale. The study used the $9\times9$ and $13\times13$ boards and did not test full-size $19\times19$ Go. The metrics already behaved differently across the two boards, with recovery time being more informative on the smaller board and the depth-based measures on the larger board. A further increase in board size could shift this balance again.

\section{Conclusions}

%% Restate the answer to RQ3 in one line [direction known, final numbers TBD]: the FuN hierarchy did not recover faster than flat PPO.
This report asked whether hierarchical feudal reinforcement learning recovers faster than flat PPO after abrupt opponent strategy shifts?. It does not. The flat PPO agent recovered faster in every condition, across both board sizes and all three shift magnitudes. On the $9\times9$ board it returned to its baseline within 6 to 22 games, whereas the Feudal Network took between 92 and 693. The larger $13\times13$ board narrowed this gap but never reversed it. Here the flat agent recovered in as few as zero games, while the hierarchy still needed up to 104. The complementary measures of disruption rate, dip depth and adaptation cost agreed with recovery time throughout. What gives the result its weight is the consistency, not any single number

%% Implication for hierarchical RL under opponent-induced non-stationarity.
The implication for hierarchical reinforcement learning is clear. The temporal abstraction that makes a manager-worker hierarchy effective for task transfer and long-horizon control does not carry over to an opponent that changes strategy. A shift of this kind disturbs the very dynamics the worker depends on, so the hierarchy cannot restrict the update to its manager. The structure intended to protect the agent instead left it vulnerable. Under opponent-induced non-stationarity of the kind examined in this study a hierarchy is not a substitute for a robust flat policy. One question this work leaves open is whether a different hierarchy could overturn this result. Design choices of the hierarchy structure shielding its worker from these shifts or correcting its manager on a faster time scale could close this gap. Until it is answered, the simpler architecture remains the stronger choice.

\section{Future work} 
%% more seeds; $19\times19$ board; a territory-margin metric. 
Several directions follow from this work. The first is to widen the seed design so that separate Phase-1 runs feed the shift experiments, which would separate the variance of the architecture from that of training. A territory or score margin could replace the binary win rate and expose disruption that the present measure absorbs. A third is to test alternative hierarchies. For instance ones that keeps the worker skills intact through a shift. This would show whether the negative result reflects hierarchy in general or the Feudal Network in particular.
